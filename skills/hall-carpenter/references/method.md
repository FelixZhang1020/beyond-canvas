# Method

**Placed where told.** No placing tool measures the hall. A part is given its heights and spans;
later parts read `hall.json`, the record of what earlier parts were *told*, never the geometry.
That is what makes a wrong number visible to the checks instead of being quietly absorbed.

**Sits, or is let in.** Timber that simply sits on timber is drawn touching it. Only a joint a
carpenter cuts to lock is drawn 2 cm into its neighbour: tie-beam tenons in the columns, the
small blocks dowelled to the arm under them, the halved crossing of two arms, posts tenoned into
the beams above and below, end purlins halved onto the long purlins, rafters notched onto their
purlins. The settle test holds pieces drawn through one another as one body. A first version let
every piece in, and the whole 3,297-piece hall settled as a single body that no gravity test
could fail; with only real joints let in it settles as 205 bodies and 58 loose blocks, the roof
(rafters, purlins, covering) the largest at 997 pieces.

**Likeness.** Every non-ground piece is drawn white through a camera with no perspective, from
the front, the side and above, inside the frame the temple was measured in. The share of the
joined outline that both buildings cover is the overlap; 0.90 in every view, and a column within
15 cm of each of the temple's columns with none extra, is "alike". The temple's own numbers,
placed by hand, score 0.96, 0.93 and 0.99.

**Built like a frame.** The outline and the columns are not enough: a box with the temple's outline
and its columns inside would pass them, and pass the gravity test. So likeness measures the hall's
frame with the survey's own functions and compares it with the temple's: every span between
neighbouring columns of a ring tied at the column heads (a timber piece over the span's middle,
reaching the column tops, running at least 0.6 of the span), the same number of roof rings, a
ridge, rafters running front to back, and a bracket set on every column head (at least three
timber pieces centred within 0.8 m, between the column top and the lowest ring). The Foguang hall
ties 36 of 36 spans and carries 18 to 37 pieces on every column head.

**Strong enough.** load-path's weights judges each column as a timber standard does: 1.3 times its
share of the hall's own weight plus 1.5 times its share of the snow on the roof, over its
cross-section, reduced for how slender it is, against the 10 MPa the lowest softwood grade is allowed
in GB 50005. A thin column fails twice over: it is small, and it bows. The Foguang hall's heaviest
column works at 4.0 MPa.

**Stands when shaken.** A post only stood on end stands for ever when it is let go in still air.
load-path's shake moves the ground like the design earthquake at the temple's site and then pulls
the hall steadily sideways as hard as that site's frequent earthquake; whatever drops, tips or ends
a metre away has come down. The cure is a carpenter's: cut the piece into what carries it, as the
ties are cut into the column heads, or make it stockier. The Foguang hall and the rebuilt hall
that was adopted both stand.

**An engineer's review, as advice.** The builder and the eyes share one model, so they can share a
mistake; the programs check only what they were written to check. `review` asks a model from another
company what does not make structural sense, from one sheet of numbers and no picture: the temple as
measured, the hall as told and as measured, the widest bays, what the columns carry, and both
tests. Piece lists and places stay off the sheet. The answer is kept to five notes, each tied to a
part the builder can change, and is never read by the hand-over gate: a model's opinion can neither
pass a hall nor fail one. Measured with NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Nano on the Spark: of eleven
notes in two rounds two were real, both since fixed, and eight misread the sheet; the two faults set
down beforehand were never raised. So it is the operator's, asked after hand-over, and a rebuild
refuses it.

**What is not claimed.** Not a structural analysis, not a piece-for-piece reconstruction, and the
measuring step knows the temple's pieces by their English names.
