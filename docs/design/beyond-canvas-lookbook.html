What Apple's glass does, and how to build each piece on the web

Your reference screenshots use this material over a dark map. Apple's own rules for it, from its design guidelines and the "Meet Liquid Glass" session, are what the studio follows. Nine behaviours make it read as glass rather than as a blurred rectangle.

LensingLight bends at the rim, so what sits behind the edge looks pulled in and slightly magnified. This is the one trait a blur cannot fake.An SVG displacement map inside backdrop-filter, as on this page, with the red, green and blue channels bent by slightly different amounts so the rim shows a faint colour fringe, the way real glass does. Chromium only; WebKit's fix was still unshipped when this page was made.
Specular highlightA bright glint moves across the surface as the device or the pointer moves, which is what makes the glass feel solid.A radial gradient on a pseudo-element whose centre follows the pointer. One listener per screen, updated once per frame.
Regular and ClearTwo materials. Regular adapts and stays legible anywhere. Clear is permanently more transparent, meant for media-rich backgrounds, and Apple says it needs a dimming layer under it. Never mix the two in one context.Two token sets; the first specimen switches between them. Text on Clear glass always sits on a scrim.
Adaptive colourThe glass takes on the colour of what is behind it, and its text flips between dark and light as the background changes.High saturation in the backdrop filter lets the paint bleed through. Over a bright painting the ink stays dark, so no flip is needed.
Concentric shapesCorners of nested elements share one centre, so a button inside a bar looks cut from the same sheet.Inner radius equals outer radius minus padding, written once as a calculation.
Morphing controlsThe selected pill in a segmented control slides and stretches to its new place instead of jumping; controls merge and split.One shared pill element and a spring-shaped easing curve. Try the Draw, Paint, Move, Story control above.
Fluid responseThings squash slightly under the finger and spring back. For children this is half the fun.The CSS linear() easing function draws a spring; Motion's real springs for anything the child drags.
Floating layerControls live in a layer above content, with a soft shadow, and shrink out of the way while the content is being used.Absolute positioning over the painting; a scroll-driven animation to shrink the bar during drawing.
No glass on glassApple's guidance is to avoid stacking glass. One layer of glass, content beneath it.The segmented control inside the top bar is a translucent track, not a second pane. Three glass panels on screen at most.

Seven directions for the studio

Each one is live. Drag, press, pick two paints. The risk dot is for a ten-day build by a small team shipping to one demo machine, with the roadshow screen at 4K.

Glass, the Apple way

The material from your reference, done to Apple's recipe: lensing at the rim, a glint that follows the pointer, paint colour bleeding through. The slider sets how thin the glass gets; Clear is Apple's preset for sitting over pictures.

Build with CSS backdrop blur and saturation, inset rim lights, and an SVG displacement map for the edge. The displacement renders in Chromium only; elsewhere the glass is frosted, not lensed.

Offline yes, pure CSS.

Ten-day risk medium. Each pane costs frame time at 4K; three on screen is the ceiling. Pin the demo box to Chrome.

Where it fits every floating control in the studio.

The picture is the room

Nothing is more inspiring to a child than their own drawing filling the screen. The interface never covers it, only floats on it, and the companion's painting arrives as a reveal the child controls.

Build with a canvas for the drawing and an image layer for the painting, split by a clip the handle moves. The generated painting streams in blurred and sharpens as it arrives.

Offline yes. The painting comes from the model on the box.

Ten-day risk medium. The reveal is a day; the model's speed decides whether the child waits. Splat's step list covers the wait.

Where it fits the centre of everything.

Colours that teach

Six paints with names a child can say, and a bowl that shows what real paint does when two meet. Yellow and blue make green here, because that is what happens on paper.

Build with a fixed table of pairs and results, not a formula. Screen colour mixing gives grey where paint gives green; the table keeps the lesson true.

Offline yes.

Ten-day risk low.

Where it fits the paint dock, and a skill of its own: "mix me a colour".

Round type, springy touch

Rounded letters, big targets, and buttons that squash and spring back. Praise arrives as a sticker, the way it does in a classroom.

Build with a rounded family bundled locally, since Safari's system rounded face is not available to Chrome. Targets at two centimetres, which research on young children's motor skills asks for, roughly three times the adult minimum.

Offline yes.

Ten-day risk low.

Where it fits every button a child presses.

Splat, the companion

The agent is a character, not a chat box. Its eyes follow the pointer, it speaks at reading pace, and it can say every line aloud, so a child who cannot read yet is never stuck.

Build with a CSS blob for the body, the system voice for speech (it works offline when the voice is installed locally), and the model's words streamed word by word.

Offline yes for speaking. Listening needs a model on the box; browsers' built-in recognition is cloud-based or experimental.

Ten-day risk low for the character, medium for two-way voice.

Where it fits beside the picture, always.

The gallery wall

Finished pictures hang on a wall with real depth; they lean as the pointer moves. A star marks the one Splat chose this week. Children come back for the wall.

Build with CSS perspective and a pointer listener. Pictures stay on the box as files; nothing uploads.

Offline yes.

Ten-day risk low.

Where it fits the home screen, and the roadshow's closing shot.

The grown-ups' room

Parents and teachers get a calm view: a few numbers, the colours learned, and a plain statement that nothing leaves the box. Same palette, no glass, no charts.

Build with plain tiles. The trail of what the companion did is the same data judges will want to see.

Offline yes.

Ten-day risk low.

Ten days, safest first

  1. Days one to three: the shell, the paint ground, the glass system with Regular and Clear, the tool rail and the dock. The studio already looks like itself.
  2. Days four to six: Splat, the painting reveal wired to the model, and voice out. This is the demo's heart, so it gets the middle of the window.
  3. Days seven and eight: the gallery wall, the grown-ups' room, the camera for paper drawings.
  4. Days nine and ten: edge refraction, the roadshow frame, one orchestrated entrance. Measure on the box at 4K and cut refraction first if frames drop.

Where the evidence cuts against the page

  • Apple's rule is no glass on glass. Your reference screenshots stack glass cards over a glass bar; the studio does not, which is why the segmented control is a translucent track and not a second pane.
  • Edge refraction renders only in Chromium; WebKit's fix was still unshipped when this page was made. On Safari the glass is frosted, not lensed. Pin the demo browser.
  • Bright paint under Clear glass fights text. Apple's own answer is a dimming layer, so every text block here sits on a scrim, and the glass is never quite as clear as the slider allows.
  • Offline listening is not a browser feature yet. It is a model on the box, and it belongs in the schedule.
  • The dark palette of the reference is gone entirely, at your request. Only its material survived.
